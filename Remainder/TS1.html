Math 252, 9.9 & 9.10, Taylor Series, Part 1 (Using the Geometric Series)

  1. 1.

    Recall. Let f be a continuously differentiable function for an interval I centered at x=0. The n-th degree Taylor polynomial, centered around x=0, was defined to be,

    pn⁢(x)=f⁢(0)+f′⁢(0)⁢x+f′′⁢(0)2!⁢x2+f′′′⁢(0)3!⁢x3+⋯+f(n)⁢(0)n!⁢xn.

    Using the graph of the polynomial, we observed as n→∞ that pn⁢(x)→f⁢(x).

    We now want to develop the machinery to prove this. In the language of series, pn⁢(x) would represent the n-th partial sum of the series. From what we have learned thus far, this means that there will be a gap between the actual sum, and the truncated sum. We called this the remainder. For polynomials, this will be another function, Rn⁢(x) to be

    Rn⁢(x)=f⁢(x)−pn⁢(x).

    This leads us to the following theorem which will give us a formula for Rn⁢(x). We will offer without proof.

    Theorem 1 (Taylor’s Theorem)

    If a function f has derivatives of all orders at x=0, then for

    pn=f⁢(0)+f′⁢(0)⁢x+⋯+f(n)⁢(0)n!⁢xn

    and

    Rn⁢(x)=f(n+1)⁢(z)(n+1)!⁢xn+1

    it follows that f⁢(x)=pn⁢(x)+Rn⁢(x). Furthermore, as n→∞, pn⁢(x)→f⁢(x), and Rn⁢(x)→0 for all x within the interval of convergence. Thus,

    f⁢(x)=∑n=0∞f(n)⁢(0)n!⁢xn

    This theorem gives us two conclusions:

    • •

      Firstly, continuously differentiable (analytic) functions can be expressed as Taylor series.

    • •

      We have equality between the two so long as the series is convergent and the remainder goes to zero.

    The next observation enables us to establish key properties of Taylor Series.

  2. 2.

    Taylor Series are Power Series. Consider a power series which, for simplicity, we will center them at x=0,

    f⁢(x)=∑n=0∞an⁢xn.

    We notice that,

    f0⁢(x) =a0+a1⁢x+a2⁢x2+a3⁢x3+a4⁢x4+…
    f1⁢(x) =a1+2⁢a2⁢x+3⁢a3⁢x2+4⁢a4⁢x3+…
    f2⁢(x) =2⁢a2+3⋅2⁢a3⁢x+4⋅3⁢a4⁢x2+…
    f3⁢(x) =3!⁢a3+4⋅3⋅2⁢a4⁢x+…
    ⋮
    fn⁢(x) =n!⁢an+(n+1)!⁢an+1⁢x+…

    Evaluating each of these derivatives, we find that

    f0⁢(0) =a0
    f1⁢(0) =1!⁢a1
    f2⁢(0) =2!⁢a2
    f3⁢(0) =3!⁢a3
    ⋮
    f(n)⁢(0) =n!⁢an

    This shows us that the constants of a power series are an=f(n)⁢(0)n!, which are precisely the constants that we had found when calculating the Taylor Polynomial. We therefore, can define what is called Taylor Series for a function which is a power series as well. In this lecture, we will focus on deriving Taylor series for functions using the Taylor Series for

    f⁢(x)=11−x

    which we know to be the sum of a Geometric series.

  3. 3.

    Example 1(Recall): Find a Taylor series centered around x=0 for

    f⁢(x)=11−x.

    Solution: Without taking derivatives, we know that a1−r=∑n=0∞a⁢rn, provided |r|<1. Thus, we can let r=x, a=1, and we find that

    11−x=∑n=0∞xn,|x|<1.

    Note, this means the radius of convergence is 1, and the series only converges to the function for values −1<x<1.

  4. 4.

    Operations of Power Series Let f and g be continuously differentiable functions on intervals around x=0. Furthermore, let an=f(n)⁢(0)n! and bn=g(n)⁢(0)n!. Then, for

    f⁢(x)=∑n=0∞an⁢xn and ⁢g⁢(x)=∑n=0∞bn⁢xn,

    we have that

    1. (a)

      For some real constant k, f⁢(k⁢x)=∑n=0∞an⁢kn⁢xn

    2. (b)

      For any integer N>0, f⁢(xN)=∑n=0∞an⁢xn⁢N.

    3. (c)

      f⁢(x)±g⁢(x)=∑n=0∞(an±bn)⁢xn where the radius of convergence is the smaller of the two radii.

  5. 5.

    Example 2: Find a Taylor Series around x=0, for f⁢(x)=4x+2.
    Solution: We first do a little manipulation on the series in order to make it resemble the sum of a geometric series,

    f⁢(x) = 4x+2
    = 42⁢(1+(x/2))
    = 21−(−x/2)

    Thus, we now have the form of the sum of a geometric series, so we let r=(−x/2), and a=2. Giving us the following

    f⁢(x) = 21−(−x/2)
    = ∑n=0∞2⋅(−x2)n
    = ∑n=0∞2⋅(−1)n⁢(xn2n)
    = ∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢21−n⁢xn

    Note this series will converge to f so long as

    |x2|<1

    which means

    |x|<2 or that −2<x<2,

    is the domain of the series.

  6. 6.

    Using Integration and Differentiation. The properties of Integration and differentiation are also conferred on Taylor Series. We can use it to determine the Taylor Series for other functions.

  7. 7.

    Example 3: Let f⁢(x)=1(1−x)2. Use the fact that

    dd⁢x⁢(11−x)=1(1−x)2

    to find a Taylor series for f around x=0.
    Solution: We know that

    11−x=∑n=0∞xn for ⁢|x|<1.

    Thus, we have that

    dd⁢x⁢(11−x) = dd⁢x⁢(∑n=0∞xn)
    = ∑n=1∞dd⁢x⁢(xn)
    = ∑n=1∞n⁢xn−1
    = ∑n=0∞(n+1)⁢xn

    Thus,

    1(1−x)2=∑n=0∞(n+1)⁢xn for ⁢|x|<1.

    Remark. In general, you will have to check that the series converges at the endpoints once you’ve either integrated or differentiated. However, in this case, we can see immediately that we won’t have convergence for either x=1 or x=−1.

  8. 8.

    Example 4: Use the fact that

    arctan⁡x=∫0x11+t2⁢𝑑t

    to find the Taylor series for f⁢(x)=arctan⁡x around x=0.
    Solution:

    We begin with the geometric-series formula

    11−x=∑n=0∞xn,|x|<1.

    Let x=−t2. Then

    11+t2=11−(−t2)=∑n=0∞(−t2)n=∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢t2⁢n,|t|<1.

    Using the given identity,

    arctan⁡x=∫0x11+t2⁢𝑑t,

    we integrate the power series term by term:

    arctan⁡x =∫0x∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢t2⁢n⁢d⁢t
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢∫0xt2⁢n⁢𝑑t
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢[t2⁢n+12⁢n+1]0x
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢x2⁢n+12⁢n+1.

    Therefore, the Taylor series for f⁢(x)=arctan⁡x centered at x=0 is

    arctan⁡x=x−x33+x55−x77+⋯=∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢x2⁢n+12⁢n+1.

    The radius of convergence is R=1. At x=1 and x=−1, the resulting alternating series also converge. Thus, the interval of convergence is

    [−1,1].
  9. 9.

    From Exact Series to Approximations.

    Thus far, we have used known power series to produce new power series representations. For example,

    arctan⁡x=x−x33+x55−x77+⋯.

    This equality involves infinitely many terms. In practice, however, we usually use only a finite number of terms:

    arctan⁡x≈x−x33+x55.

    The polynomial on the right is a partial sum of the Taylor series. The difference between the exact value and the partial sum is the remainder:

    RN=f⁢(x)−SN.

    Consequently, using a Taylor series for approximation requires us to answer two questions:

    1. (a)

      How many terms should we use?

    2. (b)

      How large can the resulting error be?

    Many of the series obtained in this lecture are alternating series. For such series, the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem gives a particularly simple error bound.

    Alternating Series Error 1

    Suppose

    ∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢an

    is an alternating series whose positive terms an decrease to 0. If

    SN=∑n=0N(−1)n⁢an,

    then

    |S−SN|≤aN+1.

    In words, the error is no greater than the magnitude of the first omitted term.

    Therefore, to approximate a value with error less than a prescribed tolerance, we choose enough terms so that

    first omitted term<desired error.
  10. 10.

    Example 5: Use a Taylor series to approximate ln⁡(1.2) with error less than 0.001.

    Solution: We begin with

    11+t=11−(−t)=∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢tn,|t|<1.

    Since

    ln⁡(1+x)=∫0x11+t⁢𝑑t,

    we integrate term by term:

    ln⁡(1+x) =∫0x∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢tn⁢d⁢t
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢∫0xtn⁢𝑑t
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢xn+1n+1.

    Therefore,

    ln⁡(1+x)=x−x22+x33−x44+⋯.

    To approximate ln⁡(1.2), let x=0.2:

    ln⁡(1.2)=0.2−(0.2)22+(0.2)33−(0.2)44+⋯.

    If we stop after the x4-term, the first omitted term has magnitude

    (0.2)55=0.000064<0.001.

    Thus,

    ln⁡(1.2) ≈0.2−(0.2)22+(0.2)33−(0.2)44
    ≈0.18227.

    Therefore,

    ln⁡(1.2)≈0.18227

    with error less than 0.001.

  11. 11.

    Example 6: Use a power series to approximate

    ∫01/211+x4⁢𝑑x

    with error less than 0.001.

    Solution: Using the geometric series,

    11+x4=11−(−x4)=∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢x4⁢n,|x|<1.

    Integrating term by term,

    ∫01/211+x4⁢𝑑x =∫01/2∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢x4⁢n⁢d⁢x
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢∫01/2x4⁢n⁢𝑑x
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢(1/2)4⁢n+14⁢n+1.

    The series begins

    12−15⋅25+19⋅29−⋯.

    After the first two terms, the first omitted term is

    19⋅29=14608<0.001.

    Therefore,

    ∫01/211+x4⁢𝑑x ≈12−15⋅25
    =12−1160
    =0.49375.

    Thus,

    ∫01/211+x4⁢𝑑x≈0.49375

    with error less than 0.001.

  12. 12.

    Example 7: Use a power series to approximate

    ∫01/2x21+x2⁢𝑑x

    with error less than 0.001.

    Solution: We know that

    11+x2=∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢x2⁢n,|x|<1.

    Multiplying by x2, we obtain

    x21+x2=∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢x2⁢n+2.

    Therefore,

    ∫01/2x21+x2⁢𝑑x =∫01/2∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢x2⁢n+2⁢d⁢x
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢∫01/2x2⁢n+2⁢𝑑x
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢(1/2)2⁢n+32⁢n+3.

    The series begins

    124−1160+1896−⋯.

    If we use the first two terms, the first omitted term is

    1896≈0.001116,

    which is not less than 0.001. We must therefore include the third term. The next omitted term is

    (1/2)99=14608<0.001.

    Hence,

    ∫01/2x21+x2⁢𝑑x ≈124−1160+1896
    ≈0.03653.

    Thus,

    ∫01/2x21+x2⁢𝑑x≈0.03653

    with error less than 0.001.
