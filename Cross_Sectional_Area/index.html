Math 252, 7.2: Volume by Area of Known Cross-Section

  1. 1.

    Motivation Consider a right rectangular prism lying along the x-axis, along the interval a≤x≤b. The cross-sections of the prism have dimensions l and w, and we know that the “height” or thickness of the prism is b−a.

    We can define the area of the cross-section , or slice of the prism, perpendicular to the x-axis, as A∗=l⁢w. Thus, we should find that the volume is

    V = l⁢w⁢h
    = l⁢w⁢(b−a)
    = A∗⁢(b−a)

    Indeed, a general principle of volume, for a solid with a constant cross-section of area A∗, can be understood as,

    V=A∗⁢h.
  2. 2.

    Extension to Other Solids Now consider a solid D has cross-sections with area A⁢(x) for a≤x≤b. We will assume A is a continuous function. Suppose we have cross-sections that traverse the solid laterally, either perpendicular to the x-axis or perpendicular to the y-axis, with known areas. These cross-sections could be squares, rectangles, triangles, semi-circles. We wish to find the volume of this solid.

    We partition the interval into n subintervals, so that the distance of each subinterval was, for 0≤k≤n

    Δ⁢x=b−an

    and

    a=x0<x1<x2<⋯<xk<⋯<xn=b

    where

    xk=a+k⁢Δ⁢x

    Consider one of the intervals, say the k-th interval [xk−1,xk]. It will have thickness Δ⁢x. What will dictate the other dimensions for a given cross-section is its location along the x-axis. So on this interval we will use a point tk in the interval, thus the cross-sectional area A depends on tk. Therefore, the volume of the slice on this interval is going to be Δ⁢V=A⁢(tk)⁢Δ⁢x. So we add all these up,

    Vn=∑k=1nA⁢(tk)⁢Δ⁢x

    This is a Riemann Sum, for which we know will converge as n→∞, Δ⁢x→0, we obtain the following formula,

    V=∫abA⁢(x)⁢𝑑x

    For solids where the cross-sections are perpendicular to the y-axis, for c≤y≤d, we find A⁢(y), and set up the formula as

    V=∫cdA⁢(y)⁢𝑑y.

    Method.

    1. (a)

      We draw a graph of the base. Do not attempt to draw a three dimensional figure. Use a straight line to depict how the cross-section lies on along the base of the solid.

    2. (b)

      Determine the dimensions of the cross-section.

    3. (c)

      Draw representative cross-section and determine a formula for its area

    4. (d)

      Set up the integral and evaluate.

    Example 1: Suppose the base of a solid is the region R, which is the region bounded between the graphs of y=1−x/2 and y=−1+x/2, and the x-axis, for x≥0. Suppose the cross-sections are equilateral triangles with the base each cross-section perpendicular to the x-axis. Determine the volume of the solid. The area of an equilateral triangle is A=34⁢s2 where s is the length of a side of the triangle.

    s⁢(x) = (1−x2)−(−1+x2)
    = 2−x

    Example 2: The base of a solid is the region bounded between the x-axis and the graph of y=x2 for 0≤x≤3. Suppose the solid has known cross-sections which are semi-circles whose diameter extends from the x-axis to the graph of y. Determine the volume of the solid.
    Solution: Sketching the region with the base on the left and the cross-section on the right:

    The base of the solid lies on the interval

    0≤x≤3.

    At a fixed value of x, the diameter of the semicircular cross-section is the vertical distance from the x-axis to the graph of y=x2. Therefore,

    d⁢(x)=x2.

    Hence the radius is

    r⁢(x)=d⁢(x)2=x22.

    The area of a semicircle is

    A⁢(x)=12⁢π⁢r⁢(x)2.

    Thus,

    A⁢(x) = 12⁢π⁢(x22)2
    = 12⁢π⋅x44
    = π8⁢x4.

    Therefore, the volume is

    V = ∫03A⁢(x)⁢𝑑x
    = ∫03π8⁢x4⁢𝑑x
    = π8⁢[x55]03
    = π8⋅2435
    = 243⁢π40.

    Example 3: Determine the volume of a right pyramid of height h, and with a square base with sides of length b.
    Solution: We draw a picture of the situation.

    The area of a square is A=s2, where s is the length of the side. If we locate a cross-section of the pyramid at some point on the y-axis, it will divide the region into a smaller triangle inside a bigger one (see the figure on the right above). Solving for all the pertinent dimensions, we find that

    h−yh=s⁢(y)b/2

    Solving for s, we get that s⁢(y)=bh⁢(h−y). Hence,

    A⁢(y)=b2h2⁢(h−y)2
    V = ∫0hb2h2⁢(h−y)2⁢𝑑y
