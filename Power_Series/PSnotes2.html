The Ratio Test and Power Series

  1. (1)

    We now turn to a new type of function called a power series. If x is a variable, then an infinite series of the form

    f⁢(x)=∑n=1∞an⁢xn=a0+a1⁢x+a2⁢x2+a3⁢x3+⋯+an⁢xn+⋯

    is called a power series. More generally,

    f⁢(x)=∑n=1∞an⁢(x−c)n=a0+a1⁢(x−c)+a2⁢(x−c)2+a3⁢(x−c)3+⋯+an⁢(x−c)n+⋯

    is called a power series centered at x=c, for some constant c.

  2. (2)

    Example 1. Consider

    f⁢(x)=∑n=0∞n!⁢xn.

    Write the first four terms of the function.
    Solution: Going through the first four values of n, we have that

    f⁢(x) = (0!⁢x0)+(1!⁢x1)+(2!⁢x2)+(3!⁢x3)+…
    = 1+x+2⁢x2+6⁢x3+…

  3. (3)

    Considerations when dealing with a function.

    1. (a)

      How to Evaluate. Since f is expressed as an infinite sum, we can plug a value into the function, but will need to determine if the series converges or diverges in order to pursue whether or not the sum exists.

    2. (b)

      What is the domain? The domain of f is the set of all x for which the series converges. This means that in order for us to find the domain of f, we need to determine when the series converges. The focus of this lecture will focus more on how we find the domain of f.

    3. (c)

      How do we do Calculus on power series? A power series is a polynomial with infinitely many terms. It is not immediately clear that the rules for differentiating and integrating automatically apply.

  4. (4)

    In order to determine the domain of f, we need to determine the values of x for which the series converges. To this end, we have either value of x such that |x|<R for which the series converges. This means that for |x|>R the series would diverge. We call R, the radius of convergence. We have the following theorem regarding this.

    Theorem.

    Let f⁢(x)=∑n=1∞cn⁢xn be a power series. Then precisely one of the following conditions is true.

    1. (a)

      The series converges at only x=0.

    2. (b)

      There exists a number R>0 such that the series converges absolutely for |x|<R (that is, for −R<x<R), and diverges for |x|>R. For x=R or x=−R, we will need to plug this into the series and determine if it converges. We call R the radius of convergence. After testing the endpoints at x=R or x=−R, we will be able to determine the interval of convergence.

    3. (c)

      The series converges absolutely for every real number x. In this case we say that the radius of convergence is R=∞.

    Note. This is all still applicable to power series centered at x=c, in the form f⁢(x)=∑n=0∞cn⁢(x−c)n. In this case, you would be looking at |x−c|<R etc.

  5. (5)

    Main Tool for Determining the Radius of Convergence. We use the Ratio Test to help us determine the radius of convergence.

    Theorem.

    Let ∑an be a series with nonzero terms, and suppose

    limn→∞|an+1an|=L
    1. (a)

      The series ∑an converges absolutely when L<1.

    2. (b)

      The series ∑an diverges when L>1.

    3. (c)

      The Ratio Test is inconclusive when L=1.

    This convergence/divergence test is applicable to numerical series that involve powers, or factorials.

  6. (6)

    Example 2: Determine the convergence or divergence of ∑n=0∞2nn!.
    Solution: Let

    an=2nn!.

    Using the Ratio Test, we have

    L =limn→∞|an+1an|
    =limn→∞2n+1(n+1)!2nn!
    =limn→∞2n+1(n+1)!⋅n!2n
    =limn→∞2n⋅2(n+1)⁢n!⋅n!2n
    =limn→∞2n+1
    =0.

    Since L=0<1, the series converges absolutely by the Ratio Test.

  7. (7)

    Example 3: Determine the convergence or divergence of ∑n=0∞n2⁢2n+13n.
    Solution: Let

    an=n2⁢2n+13n.

    We use the Ratio Test:

    L=limn→∞|an+1an|.

    First, replace n with n+1 in the formula for an:

    an+1 =(n+1)2⁢2(n+1)+13n+1
    =(n+1)2⁢2n+23n+1.

    Therefore,

    L =limn→∞|(n+1)2⁢2n+23n+1n2⁢2n+13n|.

    Dividing by a fraction is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal:

    L =limn→∞|(n+1)2⁢2n+23n+1⋅3nn2⁢2n+1|.

    Rearranging the factors gives

    L =limn→∞|(n+1)2n2⋅2n+22n+1⋅3n3n+1|.

    Now simplify each factor:

    2n+22n+1 =2(n+2)−(n+1)
    =21
    =2,

    and

    3n3n+1 =3n−(n+1)
    =3−1
    =13.

    Also,

    (n+1)2n2 =(n+1n)2
    =(nn+1n)2
    =(1+1n)2.

    Thus,

    L =limn→∞(1+1n)2⋅2⋅13
    =23⁢limn→∞(1+1n)2
    =23⁢(1)2
    =23.

    Since

    L=23<1,

    the series converges absolutely by the Ratio Test.

  8. (8)

    Example 4: Determine the convergence or divergence of ∑n=1∞(−1)n⁢nn+1
    Solution: Let

    an=(−1)n⁢nn+1.

    Using the Ratio Test, compute

    L=limn→∞|an+1an|.

    First, replace n with n+1:

    an+1 =(−1)n+1⁢n+1(n+1)+1
    =(−1)n+1⁢n+1n+2.

    Therefore,

    L =limn→∞|(−1)n+1⁢n+1n+2(−1)n⁢nn+1|.

    Dividing by a fraction is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal:

    L =limn→∞|(−1)n+1⁢n+1n+2⋅n+1(−1)n⁢n|
    =limn→∞|(−1)n+1(−1)n⋅n+1n⋅n+1n+2|.

    Simplify the alternating-sign factor:

    (−1)n+1(−1)n =(−1)(n+1)−n
    =(−1)1
    =−1.

    Thus,

    L =limn→∞|−1⋅n+1n⋅n+1n+2|
    =limn→∞n+1n⋅n+1n+2.

    Simplify the radical quotient:

    n+1n =n+1n
    =nn+1n
    =1+1n.

    Simplify the rational quotient by dividing the numerator and denominator by n:

    n+1n+2 =nn+1nnn+2n
    =1+1n1+2n.

    Therefore,

    L =limn→∞1+1n⋅1+1n1+2n
    =1+0⋅1+01+0
    =1.

    Since

    L=1,

    the Ratio Test is inconclusive. It does not determine whether the series converges or diverges.

    We therefore apply the Alternating Series Test. Let

    bn=nn+1.

    First,

    limn→∞bn =limn→∞nn+1.

    Dividing the numerator and denominator by n, we obtain

    nn+1 =nnnn+1n
    =1n1+1n.

    Thus,

    limn→∞nn+1 =01+0
    =0.

    Also, bn is eventually decreasing. Let

    f⁢(x)=xx+1.

    Using the Quotient Rule,

    f′⁢(x) =12⁢x⁢(x+1)−x(x+1)2
    =x+1−2⁢x2⁢x⁢(x+1)2
    =1−x2⁢x⁢(x+1)2.

    For x>1,

    f′⁢(x)<0.

    Therefore, bn is decreasing for n≥2, so the original series converges by the Alternating Series Test.

    To determine whether the convergence is absolute, consider

    ∑n=1∞|(−1)n⁢nn+1|=∑n=1∞nn+1.

    Use the Limit Comparison Test with

    ∑n=1∞1n.

    We have

    limn→∞nn+11n =limn→∞nn+1⋅n
    =limn→∞nn+1
    =limn→∞11+1n
    =1.

    Since

    ∑n=1∞1n

    is a divergent p-series with p=12, the absolute-value series diverges.

  9. (9)

    Strategy for Finding the Radius of Convergence (Domain). We use Ratio Test in order to determine the radius of convergence. Applying it naively to the sequential terms of the power series, we should end up with a ratio that is in terms of x. We enforce the convergence criterion that the L<1, and solve the resulting inequality for x. Keep in mind, this gives you the interior of the domain of the power series, but it does not indicate what is happening on the end points. Those must be tested separately in order to determine the interval of convergence.

  10. (10)

    Example 5: Find the radius of convergence of ∑n=0∞n!⁢xn.
    Solution: Let

    an=n!⁢xn.

    We apply the Ratio Test:

    L=limn→∞|an+1an|.

    First, replace n with n+1:

    an+1=(n+1)!⁢xn+1.

    Therefore,

    L =limn→∞|(n+1)!⁢xn+1n!⁢xn|.

    Using

    (n+1)!=(n+1)⁢n!,

    we obtain

    L =limn→∞|(n+1)⁢n!⁢xn+1n!⁢xn|
    =limn→∞|(n+1)⁢n!n!⁢xn+1xn|.

    Now simplify each quotient:

    n!n!=1

    and, for x≠0,

    xn+1xn =x(n+1)−n
    =x.

    Thus,

    L =limn→∞|(n+1)⁢x|
    =limn→∞(n+1)⁢|x|
    =|x|⁢limn→∞(n+1).

    If x≠0, then |x|>0, and therefore

    L=∞.

    Since

    L=∞>1,

    the series diverges for every x≠0.

    We must also check x=0. Substituting x=0 into the series gives

    ∑n=0∞n!⁢0n.

    The n=0 term of the original power series is

    0!⁢x0=1,

    and every term for n≥1 is zero. Thus, the series becomes

    1+0+0+0+⋯=1.

    Therefore, the series converges only when

    x=0.
  11. (11)

    Example 6: Find the radius of convergence of ∑n=03⁢(x−2)n.
    Solution: This is a geometric series of the form

    ∑n=0∞a⁢rn,

    where

    a=3andr=x−2.

    A geometric series converges when

    |r|<1.

    Therefore, this series converges when

    |x−2|<1.

    To solve the absolute-value inequality, write

    −1<x−2<1.

    Adding 2 to all three parts gives

    1<x<3.

    Thus, the series converges on the open interval

    (1,3).

    The series is centered at

    c=2,

    and the convergence condition has the form

    |x−c|<R.

    Since

    |x−2|<1,

    the radius of convergence is R=1.

  12. (12)

    Example 7: Find the radius of convergence of ∑n=0(−1)n⁢x2⁢n+1(2⁢n+1)!.
    Solution: Let

    an=(−1)n⁢x2⁢n+1(2⁢n+1)!.

    We apply the Ratio Test:

    L=limn→∞|an+1an|.

    First, replace n with n+1 to find an+1:

    an+1 =(−1)n+1⁢x2⁢(n+1)+1(2⁢(n+1)+1)!
    =(−1)n+1⁢x2⁢n+2+1(2⁢n+2+1)!
    =(−1)n+1⁢x2⁢n+3(2⁢n+3)!.

    Therefore,

    L =limn→∞|(−1)n+1⁢x2⁢n+3(2⁢n+3)!(−1)n⁢x2⁢n+1(2⁢n+1)!|.

    Dividing by a fraction is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal:

    L =limn→∞|(−1)n+1⁢x2⁢n+3(2⁢n+3)!⋅(2⁢n+1)!(−1)n⁢x2⁢n+1|.

    Rearranging the factors gives

    L =limn→∞|(−1)n+1(−1)n⋅x2⁢n+3x2⁢n+1⋅(2⁢n+1)!(2⁢n+3)!|.

    Now simplify each factor. First,

    (−1)n+1(−1)n =(−1)(n+1)−n
    =(−1)1
    =−1.

    Next,

    x2⁢n+3x2⁢n+1 =x(2⁢n+3)−(2⁢n+1)
    =x2.

    For the factorial quotient, expand (2⁢n+3)!:

    (2⁢n+3)!=(2⁢n+3)⁢(2⁢n+2)⁢(2⁢n+1)!.

    Thus,

    (2⁢n+1)!(2⁢n+3)! =(2⁢n+1)!(2⁢n+3)⁢(2⁢n+2)⁢(2⁢n+1)!
    =1(2⁢n+3)⁢(2⁢n+2).

    Substituting these simplifications into the Ratio Test gives

    L =limn→∞|−1⋅x2⋅1(2⁢n+3)⁢(2⁢n+2)|
    =limn→∞|x|2(2⁢n+3)⁢(2⁢n+2).

    Since x is fixed, |x|2 is constant. Therefore,

    L =|x|2⁢limn→∞1(2⁢n+3)⁢(2⁢n+2)
    =|x|2⁢(0)
    =0.

    Thus,

    L=0<1

    for every real number x. By the Ratio Test, the series converges absolutely for all

    −∞<x<∞.

    Therefore, the interval of convergence is

    (−∞,∞)

    and the radius of convergence is

    R=∞.

    In fact, this is the Maclaurin series for sin⁡x:

    sin⁡x=∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢x2⁢n+1(2⁢n+1)!.

    Example 8: Determine the interval of convergence of ∑n=1∞xnn.
    Solution: Let

    an=xnn.

    We begin by applying the Ratio Test:

    L=limn→∞|an+1an|.

    First, replace n with n+1:

    an+1=xn+1n+1.

    Therefore,

    L =limn→∞|xn+1n+1xnn|.

    Dividing by a fraction is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal:

    L =limn→∞|xn+1n+1⋅nxn|
    =limn→∞|nn+1⋅xn+1xn|.

    Now simplify the quotient of powers:

    xn+1xn =x(n+1)−n
    =x.

    Thus,

    L =limn→∞|nn+1⁢x|
    =|x|⁢limn→∞nn+1.

    To evaluate the remaining limit, divide the numerator and denominator by n:

    limn→∞nn+1 =limn→∞nnnn+1n
    =limn→∞11+1n
    =1.

    Therefore,

    L=|x|.

    By the Ratio Test, the series converges when

    L<1.

    Hence,

    |x|<1.

    This inequality is equivalent to

    −1<x<1.

    Thus, the radius of convergence is

    R=1.

    We must now test the endpoints x=−1 and x=1 separately.

    Testing x=1:

    Substituting x=1 gives

    ∑n=1∞1nn =∑n=1∞1n.

    This is the harmonic series, which diverges. Therefore, the series diverges at

    x=1.

    Testing x=−1:

    Substituting x=−1 gives

    ∑n=1∞(−1)nn.

    This is an alternating series. Let

    bn=1n.

    We have

    limn→∞bn=limn→∞1n=0,

    and bn is decreasing. Therefore, the series converges by the Alternating Series Test.

    However, it does not converge absolutely because

    ∑n=1∞|(−1)nn|=∑n=1∞1n,

    which is the divergent harmonic series. Thus, the series converges conditionally at

    x=−1.

    Therefore, the interval of convergence is

    [−1,1).

    The radius of convergence is

    R=1.

    Example 9: Find the interval of convergence of ∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢(x+1)n2n
    Solution: We can rewrite the general term as

    (−1)n⁢(x+1)n2n=(−x+12)n.

    Therefore, the series is geometric:

    ∑n=0∞(−x+12)n.

    A geometric series

    ∑n=0∞rn

    converges when

    |r|<1.

    In this case,

    r=−x+12.

    Thus, the series converges when

    |−x+12|<1.

    Since |−a|=|a|,

    |x+12|<1.

    Using the property

    |x+12|=|x+1|2,

    we obtain

    |x+1|2<1.

    Multiplying both sides by 2 gives

    |x+1|<2.

    Now solve the absolute-value inequality:

    −2<x+1<2.

    Subtracting 1 from all three parts gives

    −3<x<1.

    Therefore, the series converges for

    −3<x<1.

    The power series is centered at

    x=−1,

    and its radius of convergence is

    R=2.

    We must now test the endpoints x=−3 and x=1 separately.

    Testing x=−3:

    Substituting x=−3 gives

    ∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢(−3+1)n2n =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢(−2)n2n.

    Since

    (−1)n⁢(−2)n =((−1)⁢(−2))n
    =2n,

    we obtain

    ∑n=0∞2n2n=∑n=0∞1.

    This series diverges because its terms do not approach 0. Therefore, the series diverges at

    x=−3.

    Testing x=1:

    Substituting x=1 gives

    ∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢(1+1)n2n =∑n=0∞(−1)n⁢2n2n
    =∑n=0∞(−1)n.

    This series also diverges because its terms do not approach 0.

    Therefore, neither endpoint is included.

    Hence, the interval of convergence is

    (−3,1).

    The radius of convergence is

    R=2.
  15. (15)

    Calculus of Power Series: Like other types of functions, we can differentiate and integrate power series. This does not impact the radius of convergence. However, the interval of convergence is impacted.

    Theorem.

    If

    f⁢(x) = ∑n=0∞an⁢(x−c)n
    = a0+a1⁢(x−c)+a2⁢(x−c)2+⋯

    has a radius of convergence R>0, then, on the interval (c−R,c+R), we have that f is differentiable (and therefore continuous on the interval). Moreover, the derivative and antiderivative of f are as follows

    1. (a)

      f′⁢(x)=∑n=1∞n⁢an⁢(x−c)n−1

    2. (b)

      ∫0xf⁢(t)⁢𝑑t=∑n=1∞an⁢(x−c)n+1n+1

    In other words, we differentiate and integrate term by term.

    Example 10: Consider

    f⁢(x)=∑n=1∞xnn
    1. (a)

      Write the first four terms of the series, and determine the interval of convergence.

    2. (b)

      Determine the radius of convergence for f′⁢(x).

    3. (c)

      Determine the radius of convergence for ∫0xf⁢(t)⁢𝑑t

    Solution:

  17. (17)

    Consider

    f⁢(x)=∑n=1∞xnn.
    1. (a)

      Write the first four terms of the series, and determine the interval of convergence.

      Substituting n=1,2,3,4, we obtain

      f⁢(x) =x11+x22+x33+x44+⋯
      =x+x22+x33+x44+⋯.

      Thus, the first four terms are

      x+x22+x33+x44.

      To determine the interval of convergence, let

      an=xnn.

      Apply the Ratio Test:

      L=limn→∞|an+1an|.

      Since

      an+1=xn+1n+1,

      we have

      L =limn→∞|xn+1n+1xnn|
      =limn→∞|xn+1n+1⋅nxn|
      =limn→∞|nn+1⋅xn+1xn|
      =limn→∞|nn+1⁢x|
      =|x|⁢limn→∞nn+1.

      Now,

      limn→∞nn+1 =limn→∞11+1n
      =1.

      Therefore,

      L=|x|.

      The Ratio Test guarantees convergence when

      |x|<1.

      Thus, the radius of convergence is

      R=1,

      and the preliminary interval is

      −1<x<1.

      We now test the endpoints.

      At x=1,

      ∑n=1∞1nn=∑n=1∞1n,

      which is the harmonic series and therefore diverges.

      At x=−1,

      ∑n=1∞(−1)nn,

      which converges by the Alternating Series Test.

      Therefore, the interval of convergence is

      [−1,1).
    2. (b)

      Determine the radius of convergence for f′⁢(x).

      Differentiate the series term by term:

      f′⁢(x) =dd⁢x⁢(∑n=1∞xnn)
      =∑n=1∞dd⁢x⁢(xnn)
      =∑n=1∞1n⋅n⁢xn−1
      =∑n=1∞xn−1.

      Writing the first few terms gives

      f′⁢(x)=1+x+x2+x3+⋯.

      This is a geometric series with common ratio

      r=x.

      A geometric series converges when

      |x|<1.

      Therefore, the radius of convergence of f′⁢(x) is

      R=1.

      Differentiating a power series does not change its radius of convergence.

    3. (c)

      Determine the radius of convergence for

      ∫0xf⁢(t)⁢𝑑t.

      First write

      f⁢(t)=∑n=1∞tnn.

      Integrating term by term gives

      ∫0xf⁢(t)⁢𝑑t =∫0x∑n=1∞tnn⁢d⁢t
      =∑n=1∞∫0xtnn⁢𝑑t
      =∑n=1∞1n⁢[tn+1n+1]0x
      =∑n=1∞1n⁢(xn+1n+1−0n+1n+1)
      =∑n=1∞xn+1n⁢(n+1).

      Thus,

      ∫0xf⁢(t)⁢𝑑t=x22+x36+x412+x520+⋯.

      Integrating a power series does not change its radius of convergence. Therefore,

      R=1.

      In fact, the integrated series converges at both endpoints. At x=1,

      ∑n=1∞1n⁢(n+1)

      converges, and at x=−1,

      ∑n=1∞(−1)n+1n⁢(n+1)

      converges absolutely. Hence its interval of convergence is

      [−1,1].

    Example 12: Consider

Lecture Summary

Finding the Radius and Interval of Convergence

For a power series

∑n=0∞an⁢(x−c)n,

the Ratio Test is the main tool for finding the radius of convergence.

  1. (1)

    Apply the Ratio Test to the terms of the power series.

  2. (2)

    Simplify the limit and impose the condition L<1.

  3. (3)

    Solve the resulting inequality for |x−c|.

  4. (4)

    The inequality |x−c|<R gives the radius of convergence R and the open interval

    (c−R,c+R).
  5. (5)

    Test each endpoint separately to determine the interval of convergence.

Geometric-series shortcut. Before using the Ratio Test, check whether the series is geometric or can be rewritten in geometric form. For

∑n=0∞a⁢rn,

the series converges when |r|<1. This condition can sometimes be used directly to find the radius of convergence.

Endpoint reminder. The Ratio Test generally becomes inconclusive at the endpoints. Substitute each endpoint into the original series and use an appropriate convergence test, such as the Geometric Series Test, the p-Series Test, the Alternating Series Test, a Comparison Test, or the Divergence Test.

Differentiating and Integrating Power Series

Within the interval of convergence, a power series may be differentiated term-by-term:

dd⁢x⁢(∑n=0∞an⁢(x−c)n)=∑n=1∞n⁢an⁢(x−c)n−1.

It may also be integrated term-by-term:

∫∑n=0∞an⁢(x−c)n⁢d⁢x=C+∑n=0∞ann+1⁢(x−c)n+1.

Differentiation and integration do not change the radius of convergence. However, the behavior at the endpoints may change, so endpoints must be tested again when the interval of convergence is required.

Problem-Solving Checklist

  • •

  • •

  • •

  • •

  • •
